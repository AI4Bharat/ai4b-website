    A text-to-speech model that reads Indian text the way it is actually written, native script and English side by side in one sentence, in any of 45 first-party voices. Every voice speaks every language, and keeps the accent of where it comes from.

    On this page

      Indian text is code-mixed by default. A Hindi science lesson says mammal and Blue Whale in Latin script inside a Devanagari sentence; a Tamil explainer keeps alveolar capillaries in English. Most speech systems want a language flag per span and a phoneme frontend per language, and code-mixed text breaks both. We wanted a model that needs neither: give it the sentence as written, pick a voice, and it reads the whole thing.

      Indic-Speak is a 3.36B-parameter stack built around a language model whose vocabulary was extended with audio codes, so the same network that already models Hindi and English in one token stream also models how they sound. The voices are learned from people, and they carry where they are from: a Kashmiri voice reading Tamil sounds like a Kashmiri speaker reading Tamil. For some products that is exactly what you want, and for the rest it is the thing to know before you choose.

      Writing for the ear

      A model that reads text as written has to be handed text a person would actually say out loud. Nobody says "one two three comma four five six point seven eight rupees", and nobody says "backslash int". The normaliser in front of the model turns written notation into spoken words first, and it is the part of the stack that does the least glamorous and most necessary work.

      03 How it is built

      A text decoder whose vocabulary was extended with audio codes, served as a streaming endpoint. There is no phoneme frontend, no duration predictor and no separate prosody model anywhere in the stack. What follows is the whole path from your sentence to a waveform.

      model

      Llama-3.2-3B backbone

      Parameters — 3.301 B in the backbone; 3.359 B across everything that runs at inference, counted per component below
      Vocabulary — 156,960 tokens: 128,256 text plus 28,672 audio codes and a small set of speaker, style and non-verbal tokens, with input and output embeddings tied
      Audio frame — 7 codec tokens per 85.33 ms frame, 82.03 tokens per second of speech
      Context — 131,072 positions, so length is a budget rather than a limit

      audio

      SNAC codebooks, Vocos decoder

      Codebooks — SNAC's quantizer at 24 kHz, three codebooks at a 1:2:4 time ratio flattened into one token stream. Only the codebooks are used, to turn generated codes back into latents: 0.14 M parameters
      Decoder — those latents go straight to a fine-tuned Vocos decoder, 58.4 M parameters. SNAC's own encoder and decoder are never in the path
      Output — 16-bit PCM at 24 kHz, streamed as it is generated

      serving

      One request, streamed

      Request — text, voice, optional style, optional sampling settings; the language is inferred from the text
      Length — about 30 s of speech per request, a 2,520-token ceiling; a request takes the text you give it, and splitting longer passages is the caller's job
      Defaults — temperature 0.6, top-p 0.9, top-k 50, repetition penalty 1.2. The conservative end of the sweep, chosen for the lowest defect rate; the livelier settings carry several times the failure rate and vary more between seeds

      What the model is

      Indic-Speak is a Llama-3.2-3B decoder that had 28,672 audio codes added to its vocabulary and was then taught to continue a written sentence with sound instead of more words. That is the entire idea. There is no acoustic model, no phoneme dictionary, no aligner, and no duration predictor: the network that already knew how Hindi and English fit together in one sentence was given a way to say them.

      Because the audio codes are ordinary vocabulary entries, everything the decoder already did keeps working on them. Attention reaches back over the whole utterance, so a clause can be shaped by a comma thirty words earlier. The 131,072-position context makes a long passage a budget question rather than an architectural one. And the same sampling controls that steer text, temperature and top-p and a repetition penalty, steer the voice.

      You are given three levers and no others: the speaker, an optional style, and the punctuation of your text. Emotion, pace and emphasis are downstream of those three. It is a small surface, and it is small on purpose.

      Why a language-model backbone

      The alternative was a purpose-built acoustic model with a phoneme frontend. A text decoder buys three things this product depends on. Code-mixing comes for free, because the backbone already models Hindi and English in one token stream and needs no language decision per span. Punctuation becomes prosody, because the model already knows a comma is a boundary. And long context makes a multi-minute utterance a sequence-length question rather than an architectural one.

      Generation from a language model is sampled rather than deterministic, which is what makes it expressive and also what makes it worth measuring properly. A rare bad sample sounds like speech, so a signal-quality score would miss it. That is why the evaluation below reads transcripts and judges content, and why we publish the whole distribution rather than only the mean.

      04 How it measures up

      Fifteen thousand code-mixed sentences across ten languages, each read twice by different voices: thirty thousand readings spread over all 45 voices, mostly cross-lingual casting. Every reading was transcribed by an Indic speech recogniser, then scored by an independent judge model on a 0 to 5 content-fidelity scale that treats script, spelling and number-format differences as correct.

      Why not word error rate? The recogniser writes English words in native script, so mammal in a Bengali sentence comes back as ম্যামাল and counts as an error against the Latin-script source even when it was spoken cleanly. Across the readings in the judge's top band, string word error rate still averages 48.8 and character error rate 52.9: on code-mixed text those numbers measure the script mismatch, not the speech. We keep both in the data and let the judge give the verdict.

      05 Where it falls short today

      • 010.7% of readings need a second take. The model repeats a phrase, drops a clause, or lets a number drift. These look like sampling accidents rather than a limit of the model: in an earlier study, re-generating 56 such rows recovered 55 of them, 50 of those on the first retry. A pipeline that transcribes its own output and retries the outliers is the pattern we would build.
      • 02Three voices are noticeably weaker. Sansuma (4.53), Gwrbw (4.74) and Madhukar (4.76) trail the other 42, which sit between 4.82 and 4.96. Sansuma reading Marathi is the one voice-and-language pair below 4.1.
      • 03Style tags must match exactly. children's stories works; Children's Stories comes back as a 422. Emotion tags are lowercase, the customer-care tag is title case.
      • 04One request is about 30 seconds. Longer passages are produced by splitting at punctuation and joining the pieces, which is what the audiobook chapter above does. Single-pass multi-minute generation is in progress.
      • 05Numbers default to English. Latin digits inside an Indic sentence are read as English words unless you pass the sentence language to the normaliser or write native-script digits.
      • 06Accent travels with the voice. This is by design, but it means the recommended native voice is the safe default for any product where the listener expects a local speaker.
